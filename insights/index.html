01 / ACCOUNT SECURITY

6 MIN READ

Better passwords and stronger sign-ins

A password is one part of protecting an account. A strong setup makes passwords hard to guess, keeps them unique, and adds another check when someone signs in.

Make every password long and unique

Use a different password for every work account. A password manager can generate and store long, random passwords, so people don’t have to reuse a familiar one. Protect the password manager itself with a strong master passphrase and MFA.

Turn on MFA for important accounts

Multi-factor authentication asks for an extra proof of identity in addition to a password. Enable it first on email, administrator accounts, file storage, financial tools, and remote access. Use the strongest method available in your system, such as a passkey, security key, or authenticator app. Keep recovery options current and store backup codes securely.

A good first step: Check whether your business email and administrator accounts have MFA turned on. Then work through the rest of your critical services.

For plain-language guidance, see CISA’s password guidance and MFA guidance.

02 / DATA PROTECTION

5 MIN READ

Protect business data, every day

Customer records, invoices, contracts, staff information, and working files all deserve care. You can reduce avoidable exposure by knowing what you keep and giving people access only to what they need.

Know where important information lives

Make a simple list of the places your business stores important data: email, shared drives, laptops, cloud services, and business applications. Know who owns each service and who can access it. Remove access when people change roles or leave.

Use everyday safeguards

  • Keep operating systems, apps, browsers, and devices up to date.
  • Use MFA and unique passwords for business accounts.
  • Limit administrator access to people who need it for a specific task.
  • Lock devices when unattended and encrypt devices that hold sensitive work.
  • Teach staff to pause over unexpected links, attachments, and urgent requests for money or credentials.

Review file sharing settings now and then. Check that sensitive folders are shared with the right people, external sharing is intentional, and former staff no longer have access.

Keep it manageable: Start with your email, financial accounts, customer information, and the devices people use to reach them.

CISA’s Secure Our World resources cover strong passwords, MFA, software updates, and phishing awareness.

03 / BACKUP & RECOVERY

6 MIN READ

Backups only count if you can restore them

Accidental deletion, a failed device, ransomware, or a damaged office can all make business information unavailable. A backup plan is a practical part of being ready to recover.

Decide what needs to come back first

List the files and systems your team would need to keep operating: shared documents, finance and customer records, email, key configurations, and essential applications. Choose how often each should be backed up based on how much recent work your business could afford to lose.

Keep a protected copy and check it

Use an automated backup approach for critical data. Keep a separate copy away from everyday access where feasible, so a problem affecting your main systems cannot simply reach every copy. Set a reminder to check backup status and investigate failures.

Most of all, test restoring a few files and a business-critical system. Confirm that the restored information is complete, opens correctly, and can be accessed by the right people. Record who can perform a recovery and where the instructions are kept.

Try a small restore test: Pick a non-critical folder, restore a few files to a separate location, and verify they open. Schedule larger recovery exercises around your business needs.

CISA recommends protecting critical backups from network access and testing that recovery works. See its data backup guidance.

04 / EVERYDAY AWARENESS

4 MIN READ

Pause before you trust an unexpected message

Some fraudulent messages look routine. They may copy a supplier, coworker, or familiar service and try to rush you into opening a file, sharing a password, or changing payment details.

Watch for the moment of pressure

Unexpected urgency, unusual payment instructions, unfamiliar links, and requests to bypass normal process are reasons to slow down. Check the sender carefully, but don’t rely on the displayed name alone.

Verify through a separate route

If a message asks for a payment change or sensitive information, contact the person or company using a phone number or address you already trust—not the details in the message. Report suspicious mail using your organization’s process, then delete it.

Make it easy to report: Let staff know who to contact when a message feels off, and reassure them that raising a concern quickly is the right thing to do.

See CISA’s practical advice for recognizing and reporting phishing.
